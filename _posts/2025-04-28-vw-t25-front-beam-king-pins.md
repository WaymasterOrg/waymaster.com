## The Clonk

Every T25 owner knows the clonk. It's a low-frequency knock from the front end on bumps, pot holes and speed humps — the kind of sound that makes passengers grip their armrests and ask if the car is safe to drive.

On the Volkswagen T25, the front suspension uses king pins and link pins rather than the ball joints found on most modern vehicles. It's a design carried over from the earlier air-cooled buses, and it's fundamentally sound — but when the king pins wear, the consequences are more severe than on a ball-joint suspension. The wheel can move in ways it shouldn't, the steering becomes vague and wandery, and eventually you start questioning your insurance cover.

## What I Found

The T25 went onto axle stands for an inspection. With the wheel off and a helper rocking the steering, the wear was immediately apparent — more than a millimetre of play in the king pin, with a noticeable clunk at the top of its travel.

Standard repair: source a king pin kit (pins, bearings, thrust washers, seals), press out the old pins, press in new ones. It's a half-day job with the right tools.

But while the beam was accessible, I ran a light along it. There was a hairline crack in the front beam itself — specifically at the weld junction of the lower arm mount on the nearside. It wasn't visible without good lighting and a close look. The beam was structurally compromised.

This changes the job.

## Sourcing a Replacement Beam

T25 front beams come up occasionally at breakers and on the forums. A solid, uncracked unit was sourced from a scrapped 1986 T25 — same suspension geometry, compatible without modification.

The condition was inspected before purchase: no cracks, no distortion, the torsion bar pockets undamaged. The arm pivot bushes were worn but serviceable until next year.

## The Removal

Removing the front beam from a T25 is not a quick job but it's straightforward if you work methodically:

1. Disconnect the brake lines and support the callipers
2. Remove the brake drums and hubs
3. Disconnect the steering column from the steering box
4. Disconnect the tie rods
5. Support the beam and remove the four mounting bolts (two front, two rear)
6. Lower the beam on the jack and manoeuvre it out

The cracked beam came out cleanly. The crack, once visible in good light on the bench, was clearly propagating — it would have failed within another year of use.

## The Rebuild

Before fitting the replacement beam, the king pins and bearings were replaced with a fresh kit. This is easier off the van on a bench:

- King pins pressed out with a hydraulic press (don't try this with hammer and drift — you'll distort the eye)
- Bearing housings cleaned and inspected
- New bearings, seals and thrust washers fitted
- King pins pressed in to the correct depth
- Greased through the nipples until clean grease emerges at each seal

The replacement beam was painted with chassis black before fitting, and the brake lines were replaced with new copper-nickel tubing rather than reconnecting the old steel lines.

## The Result

The clonk is gone. The steering is direct and communicative in a way it hadn't been since I'd owned the van. The front end is solid.

Total cost: beam (£65 from breaker), king pin kit (£45), brake tubing and fittings (£30), new boots and seals throughout (£25). Call it £165 and a weekend.

## One More Thing

While the beam was out and the front end was accessible, I took the opportunity to inspect the steering box. There's a small weep at the input shaft seal — not urgent, but noted. It'll be attended to before the next long trip.

The T25's electrical rewire is next on the list, and with it the initial Waymaster hardware installation. The front end being sorted first means we're building on solid foundations.
